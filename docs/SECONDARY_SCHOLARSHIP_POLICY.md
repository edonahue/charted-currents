# Secondary scholarship policy

Charted Currents relies heavily on structured datasets, archival catalogues, primary records, maps, and other direct historical evidence. Those sources can establish many facts, but they do not by themselves justify every explanatory statement the product may want to make.

Historical interpretation should be informed by relevant secondary scholarship rather than generated from plausible prose alone.

## Primary and secondary roles are different

A primary or near-primary record may establish that:

- a vessel appears in a register;
- a person is recorded as master;
- a fleet has a particular title;
- an event occurred on a documented date;
- a map labels a place.

Secondary scholarship may help support claims about:

- institutional significance;
- historical context;
- historiographical interpretation;
- economic/social meaning;
- legal practice;
- imperial structures;
- causes and consequences;
- contested terminology;
- why a pattern matters.

Do not make a primary source carry interpretive weight it was not designed to support.

## Interpretive prose requires a support path

For public historical prose, distinguish at least conceptually:

### Evidence description

States what a source or set of sources records.

Example:

> Crespo records the 1706 vessel as part of a fleet bound for Nueva España.

This may be supported directly by source assertions and deterministic joins.

### Project methodology

Explains what Charted Currents did.

Example:

> Charted Currents groups these occurrences as a probable person match because four compatible master-name records recur between 1688 and 1706.

This is a statement about project method and should point to the resolution evidence.

### Historical interpretation

Explains significance beyond the direct record.

Example:

> The convoy illustrates a broader institutional strategy for protecting transatlantic commerce.

This normally requires appropriate secondary scholarship or a clearly documented analytical argument.

### Causal interpretation

Claims that one historical factor caused or materially drove another.

This carries the highest burden and should follow Class G rules in `docs/HISTORICAL_ASSERTION_POLICY.md`.

## If the interesting sentence is unsupported, omit it

Generative models are especially good at writing plausible historical context that sounds authoritative.

Agents must not treat stylistic plausibility as evidence.

When source records only support a descriptive statement, prefer accurate descriptive prose to speculative narrative enrichment.

Silence is preferable to polished misinformation.

## Secondary-source registry

The interstitial scholarly-review implementation should evaluate a small source-of-truth structure for secondary scholarship rather than scattering citations through prose.

A useful record may include:

- stable project ID;
- author/editor;
- title;
- publication/journal/publisher;
- year;
- DOI/ISBN/stable URL where applicable;
- relevant pages/sections when a specific claim depends on them;
- topic tags;
- access/notes where relevant;
- claims or interpretive topics supported.

Do not create a large bibliographic system before the product needs it. The key requirement is that interpretive claims can be traced to actual scholarship.

## Do not launder primary citations through secondary works

If a secondary work cites an archival record that Charted Currents has not independently inspected, do not cite the archival record as though the project examined it.

Instead preserve the chain:

`secondary work → cites archival record`

and describe the inspection state honestly.

The archival citation may be a useful research lead. It is not direct project inspection until the underlying source is actually examined.

## Review of secondary-supported claims

For new Class E/G public claims, reviewers should ask:

- Does the cited work actually support the precise statement?
- Are page/section references specific enough to retrace the claim?
- Is the source being represented fairly rather than cherry-picked?
- Does another major interpretation materially complicate the statement?
- Is the prose stronger or more causal than the scholarship?
- Is a historiographical disagreement being presented as settled fact?
- Does the scholarship concern the same time/place/institution as the claim?

## Scholarly disagreement

Charted Currents does not need to adjudicate every scholarly debate.

When a material disagreement exists, the project may:

- state the bounded fact and omit the disputed interpretation;
- summarize competing views with citations;
- label a project synthesis as interpretation;
- keep the issue in a research/review queue.

Do not flatten disagreement into false certainty for cleaner UX.

## Older scholarship and source criticism

Age alone does not make scholarship unusable, especially for specialized archival work, but agents should remain alert to:

- outdated terminology;
- superseded factual claims;
- colonial/racial assumptions embedded in older historiography;
- archival discoveries unavailable to earlier authors;
- later corrections or major reinterpretations.

Use the strongest appropriate scholarship available for the claim rather than defaulting to the first accessible text.

## AI summaries of scholarship

AI may summarize a legitimately accessed secondary source for internal research and may propose how it bears on a project claim.

Before publication of a consequential interpretive claim:

- verify the source text/metadata actually supports the summary;
- preserve citation details;
- do not convert a model's synthesis into an untraceable historical statement.

## Attribution and intellectual debt

Charted Currents should visibly acknowledge scholarship that materially shapes public interpretations, terminology, source discovery, or methodology.

A citation is not only a legal/formatting obligation. It is part of the evidentiary trail that allows another reader to retrace the argument.

## Scope discipline

Do not turn every inspector panel into an essay.

Secondary scholarship should deepen claims where interpretation materially improves understanding. It should not become decorative citation density or delay routine publication of well-supported descriptive facts.
